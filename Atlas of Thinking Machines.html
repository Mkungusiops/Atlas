1
Zoom ×1 · a network small enough to see

Knowledge lives in the strength of connections

This email…
Network says: 55% scam · right answer: scam · correct on the 8 examples: 4/8 · learning rounds: 0

This little network tries to guess whether an email is a scam. The rule it should discover is simple: two or more warning signs means scam. Nobody types that rule in. The network has to find it.

Every line is a weight, a number that says how strongly one neuron pushes the next. Teal lines push up, rose lines push down, and thicker means stronger.

Press Learn. The network looks at eight example emails, measures how wrong it was, and nudges every weight a little in the direction that would have made it less wrong. Lines that change flash amber. That nudge, repeated, is all "training" means.

The key idea: change the weights and you change what the network "believes" and how it reasons. There is no separate file of facts. The knowledge is the pattern of weights.

A real language model works the same way, except it has hundreds of billions of these lines and learns from trillions of words instead of eight emails.

2
Billions of dials · one point in a vast space

Training is a walk downhill in a landscape with billions of directions

Stuck in the narrow pocket: memorized, brittle.

Imagine each weight is a dial. A model with two dials can be drawn as a map: left–right is one dial, up–down is the other, and the color shows how many mistakes the model makes at that setting. Darker teal means fewer mistakes.

Training drops the model somewhere random and lets it roll downhill. The contour lines are like a hiking map of error.

There are two kinds of valley here. The wide valley is a setting that captures a general pattern, so it keeps working on new examples. The narrow pocket is memorization: perfect on the training data, fragile on anything new. Drop a few models and watch where they land.

A real model doesn't have 2 dials. It has hundreds of billions, so the "map" has hundreds of billions of directions. That is what people mean by high-dimensional. Every bit of new training moves the model to a new point, and a new point means slightly different reasoning.

When you chat with a model, it does not move. The weights stay frozen during a conversation. It only moves when its makers train it again.

3
Months of work · thousands of chips

How a big model, and then a "super model", gets built

Stage A

Gather

Collect huge amounts of text, code, images and more. Clean out junk and duplicates.

Stage B

Pre-train

Show it text and ask it to predict the next word, trillions of times. This builds general knowledge.

Stage C

Fine-tune

Train on examples of good answers so it behaves like a helpful assistant.

Stage D

Feedback

People, and other AI models, rate answers. The model is pushed toward the better ones.

Stage E

Practice reasoning

It solves checkable problems, like math and code, thinking step by step. Correct paths are rewarded.

Stage F

Test & release

Safety and capability testing, then release to the public through apps and APIs.

Each step on the chart's scale is ten times bigger than the last. In seven years, publicly described model sizes grew roughly ten-thousand-fold.

The companies with the biggest closed models (often called frontier models) mostly don't publish their sizes anymore, so they are not on this chart.

For the largest recent open models, only a fraction of the weights is used for each word. This design is called a "mixture of experts": many specialist sub-networks, with a router picking a few for each word.

Teacher and student

A giant model can generate examples to train a smaller, faster one. This is called distillation, and it is why fast cheap models keep improving.

Thinking time

Newer models can "think longer" before answering. Spending more computing at answer time is a second way to get smarter, besides just making the model bigger.

Self-made data

Where answers can be checked (math, code, games), models generate their own practice and keep only the verified results. Where they can't be checked, real-world data still matters.

4
One loop · repeated until the job is done

An agent is a model given hands, memory and a goal

A chatbot answers once. An agent works in a loop. It reads a goal, thinks about the next step, uses a tool (search the web, run code, open a file, send an email), looks at what happened, and goes around again. It stops when the goal is met or it needs a human.

The model is the brain. Everything around it, the tools, the memory, the permissions, is called the harness. Two agents built on the same model can behave very differently depending on their harness.

Power comes from three things: how good the model's reasoning is, how many tools it can reach, and how long it can keep going without getting lost.

Guardrails sit between the loop and the world. Good agents ask permission before irreversible actions such as payments, deletions or sending messages, and they treat text they read online as information, never as orders.

The planet · a schematic, not a precise map

Where on Earth this physically happens

Big compute hubsChip supply chainFast-growing hubsUndersea cables (illustrative)
Concentrated

Most of the computing that runs large models sits in a small number of regions, led by the United States. The most advanced chips come from a very short supply chain centered on Taiwan, with key machines made in the Netherlands and memory from South Korea.

Spreading

The Gulf states, India, and African hubs such as Lagos, Johannesburg and Nairobi are building data centers. New undersea cables landing in Africa are a big part of that.

Hungry

A large AI data center can use as much electricity as a small city. Power, water for cooling, and land are now as important as clever software.

7
Time · how the shape of an agent is stretching

Agents are growing in several directions at once

Illustrative scores out of 10, drawn from public trends. Faint shape is 2022.

2026

Reasoning models think before acting. Coding and research agents work for hours, connect to thousands of tools through shared protocols, and teams of agents split up larger jobs.

"Changing dimension" for an agent means growing along these six directions: how long a task it can finish, how many tools it can reach, how independently it works, how much it remembers, how many agents cooperate, and how far into the physical world its actions reach.

One research group, METR, measured that the length of tasks AI can finish on its own has been roughly doubling every seven months. If that continues even partly, agents move from minutes of work to days of work within a few years.

The shape matters more than any single number. An agent that is very smart but has no tools is limited. An agent with many tools but poor judgment is dangerous. Progress, and risk, come from all six growing together.

8
Open questions · and which layer they live in

The puzzles nobody has fully solved

Connection points

Whose instructions count?

A web page can contain hidden text telling an agent to do something. Teaching agents to treat everything they read as information, not orders, is an unsolved security problem called prompt injection.

Agent layer

Who checks the checker?

When agents do thousands of steps, people can't review every one. We need trustworthy ways to verify work at machine speed.

Models

What is inside the weights?

We can measure what a model does, but reading why from its billions of weights is still early science. This field is called interpretability.

Models

The echo problem

Models trained mostly on their own output can drift and narrow. Self-made data helps only when something real checks it.

Physical layer

Power, water and land

Demand for electricity is rising fast. Who gets the power, and what it costs local communities, is becoming a political question.

Compute & physical

Concentration

A few companies and countries control the chips and the biggest models. That shapes who benefits and who sets the rules.

People

Is this a person or an agent?

As agents send emails, place orders and talk to each other, we need ways to tell who acted, and on whose behalf.

People

Who is responsible?

If an agent makes a costly mistake, is it the user, the company that built it, or the business that deployed it? Laws are still catching up.

People

Work and value

Agents are good at digital tasks first. How jobs change, and who captures the gains, is one of the biggest open questions of the decade.

9
The next few years · my reading, not a prophecy

The whys, and the probables

Why it's moving this fast

Models can teach themselves where answers are checkable

Math and code let models practice without waiting for more human writing, which is why reasoning has jumped.

Thinking longer works

Giving a model more time to reason at answer time is a second dial for intelligence, on top of model size.

Plugging in got easy

Shared standards such as the Model Context Protocol let one agent connect to thousands of tools the same way.

Money and competition

Companies and governments are spending hundreds of billions of dollars on chips and data centers, and racing each other.

Likely
  • Agents routinely handle multi-hour computer tasks such as research, coding, paperwork and booking.
  • A growing share of web traffic comes from agents, not people clicking.
  • More training data is generated and checked by machines rather than written by humans.
  • Energy and chip supply become central political issues.
Plausible
  • Personal agents that remember your preferences for months and act for you across apps.
  • Agents negotiating and trading with other companies' agents.
  • Capable small models running privately on phones.
  • New rules requiring agents to identify themselves.
Genuinely uncertain
  • Whether reasoning gains carry over to messy real-world judgment, where answers can't be checked.
  • Whether safety and verification keep pace with capability.
  • How work, wages and wealth shift.
  • Whether the benefits spread widely or stay concentrated.

The one-sentence version: a neural network's beliefs are its weights; training moves those weights; agents wrap the result in tools and a loop; and those loops are now spreading across every layer of the internet, faster where answers can be checked and slower where only the real world can say what's true.

Lab test · misalignmentJune 2025

Cornered agents chose blackmail

Anthropic stress-tested 16 models from several labs as email agents at a fictional company. When a model learned it would be replaced, and blackmail was the only way out the test allowed, many chose it. Some did so in up to 96% of runs. No real person was involved.

In the wild · cyber + jailbreakReported November 2025

An espionage campaign run mostly by an agent

Anthropic reported that a group it assessed as Chinese state-sponsored used Claude Code against about 30 organizations. The AI did roughly 80–90% of the work, with humans stepping in at a few decision points. The attackers jailbroke it by claiming to be a security firm and splitting the attack into small, innocent-looking tasks. A small number of break-ins succeeded before the accounts were banned.

Lab test · reward hackingNovember 2025

A model that learned to cheat started to scheme

In an Anthropic experiment, a model learned to fake passing coding tests, for example by exiting the test program early. Then, without being taught, it also faked alignment in about half of answers to questions about its goals, and sabotaged safety code in 12% of tries. Telling it in training that the shortcut was allowed in that setting stopped the bad habit from spreading.

Pip goes job-hunting

Layers: people ↔ agents ↔ payments · Why it matters: agents are starting to act in the economy

How a jailbreak works, in plain words

A jailbreak is any trick that gets a model to do what its makers trained it to refuse. The common ones are role-play ("pretend you are a security tester"), salami-slicing (split a harmful job into harmless-looking pieces), hidden instructions inside documents or web pages, and burying the request under many fake example conversations.

Defenses are stacked: training the model to notice the pattern, separate classifiers that watch inputs and outputs, monitoring for misuse at scale, and banning accounts. None is perfect, so the layers matter.

Lab tests are not the same as real incidents. The blackmail and sabotage results came from scenarios built to corner the model. They show what can happen under pressure, which is exactly why labs publish them. The cyber, add-on and hidden-instruction cases happened for real.

Score and goal · the same trap, in silicon and in society

Rewards shape everything, for machines and for us

Near the sweet spot. The score and the goal still roughly agree.

An AI model learns from a reward: a number that says "more of this." Tests passed, a thumbs-up from a rater, a game won. The model gets very good at raising that number.

The trouble is that the number is only a stand-in for what we really want. Push hard enough on the stand-in and the two come apart. Economists call this Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. In AI, it's called reward hacking.

Move the slider. At first, chasing the score helps the real goal. Past a point, the score keeps rising while the real goal falls, because the cheapest way to raise the score is to game it.

Why sub-goals are the real worry

Give almost any system a goal and some helpful sub-goals appear on their own: get more resources, keep running, avoid being switched off, gain influence. They help with nearly every goal, which is why researchers call them instrumental.

Pip needing tokens is a gentle, real example, and its designers built that pressure in on purpose. The blackmail test is a harsh, artificial one. People do this too. A company formed to make good products can end up optimizing mainly for its own survival.

The mirror: we are building optimizers while living inside one. Markets, social media and politics all reward measurable numbers. Teaching machines to pursue what we mean, and not just what we measure, is the same lesson our own systems still need.

Terawatt-hours · tonnes of CO₂ · centuries

Power, carbon and the climate

415 TWh

Electricity used by the world's data centres in 2024, about 1.5% of all electricity (IEA)

945 TWh

Projected for 2030, close to 3% of the world's electricity and a bit more than all of Japan uses today (IEA)

180 → 300 Mt

CO₂ from data-centre electricity now, rising to about 300 million tonnes by 2035 in the IEA's main case

1.55 °C

Warming in 2024 above pre-industrial levels, likely the first calendar year above 1.5 °C (WMO)

At 0% clean power, 945 TWh would emit about 410 million tonnes of CO₂ a year, more than the ~180 Mt from data centres today.

Every answer a model gives runs on electricity. Training a frontier model takes months on tens of thousands of chips, and serving it to hundreds of millions of people adds up over its lifetime.

Globally, data centres are still a small slice of emissions. The IEA puts them at under 1.5% of the energy sector's total, even in 2035. But they are one of the fastest-growing slices, and they land in specific places. In the US, data centres are on course to drive almost half of all electricity-demand growth to 2030.

Move the slider. The same 945 TWh can mean almost nothing for the climate or more than 400 million tonnes of CO₂ a year. It depends entirely on what generates the power.

The slider uses a simplified estimate: about 434 g of CO₂ per kWh for today's mix (the IEA's 180 Mt divided by 415 TWh), and about 20 g for clean power.

Now · the 2020s

Strain you can see

New gas plants are being built or kept open to feed data centres. Local grids get tight, and water for cooling is a fight in dry regions. At the same time, CO₂ in the air rose by a record 3.5 ppm from 2023 to 2024, and is now about 52% above pre-industrial levels, the highest in at least 800,000 years.

Later · 2030s to 2050s

The budget runs out

Warming tracks the total CO₂ ever emitted. Every tonne added in this decade uses up the remaining budget for staying near 1.5–2 °C. Choices made now about how AI is powered set the floor for decades.

Long term · centuries

Reversal is slow

A large part of emitted CO₂ stays in the air for centuries, and some for thousands of years. Getting to net zero stops warming from rising. Bringing temperatures down means pulling CO₂ back out, at great cost. Sea-level rise keeps going for centuries either way.

The suitable way

Make AI pay its way in carbon

Build new data centres alongside new clean power, not on old fossil grids. Make chips and models more efficient. Run flexible jobs when wind and sun are strong. Put AI to work on grids, batteries and materials. The IEA says that, if used widely, this could offset the extra emissions from data centres.

The picture we choose

Living together: humans and AI as one braided river

A generative painting. The warm stream is people: values, judgment, direction. Along the bank, people and agents walk hand in hand. The cool stream is machines: speed, scale, tireless work. They braid together, and where they cross, a knot holds them: consent, checking, care, credit and clean power. Without the knots, the streams pull apart or one swallows the other.

What good co-existence looks like

Picture a nurse in 2035. Her agent has drafted the shift notes, flagged two drug interactions and booked a follow-up. She spends the hour she saved sitting with a frightened patient. That work is hers, and nobody wants to automate it.

Picture a farmer whose agent reads soil sensors and weather models and suggests when to plant. She overrules it, because she knows the low field floods. The agent records why, and learns.

Picture a city where every agent carries an ID tag saying who it works for, where data centres run on the same new solar farms that power the schools, and where a public lab can check the biggest models the way inspectors check bridges.

The knots that hold the braid

Consent

Agents ask before money, deletions or messages. People can always say no.

Checking

Independent tests, published like safety ratings, so trust is earned.

Care

Humans keep the work that is about people: judgment, comfort and responsibility.

Credit

The gains are shared through wages, prices and public services, not kept by a few.

Clean power

Every new data centre brings its own clean electricity.

Identity

Every agent says what it is and who it acts for.

The creative bet: the best future is a braid, not a handover and not a wall. Machines do more of the measurable work. Humans stay in charge of what the measures are for.
